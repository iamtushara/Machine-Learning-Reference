K-Nearest Neighbours, Interactively

KNN is the "ask your neighbours" algorithm. It doesn't really learn anything up front — it just memorises the training data. To classify a new point, it finds the k closest known points and takes a vote. Click anywhere on the canvas to drop a mystery point and watch it happen.

2. How it actually works

  1. Store everything. Training is just saving the labelled points. This is why KNN is called a "lazy learner".
  2. Measure distance. For a new point, compute distance to every stored point — usually Euclidean: √((x₁−x₂)² + (y₁−y₂)²).
  3. Sort and take k. Keep only the k smallest distances.
  4. Vote. The most common class among those k neighbours wins. (For regression, average their values instead.)

Choosing k

Small k (1–3) hugs the data tightly and is noisy — one mislabelled point can flip a prediction. Large k smooths the boundary but can wash out small, genuine clusters. Odd values avoid ties in two-class problems. Try dragging the slider near the boundary and watch the answer flip.

Why scaling matters

Distance is blind to units. If one feature is "salary in £" (0–100,000) and another is "years of experience" (0–40), salary completely dominates the distance. Always normalise or standardise features first.

The trade-off

Training costs nothing; prediction costs everything. Every query compares against the whole dataset — O(n) per prediction. It also degrades in high dimensions, where all points become roughly equidistant (the curse of dimensionality).

Try it: switch to "Add mode: Cats" and plant a few cats deep inside dog territory. Then set k=1 and k=15 on a nearby query point — you'll see exactly how k controls sensitivity to noise.
